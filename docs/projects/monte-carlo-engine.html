Monte Carlo Engine

Lately I've been working on a Monte Carlo simulation engine in modern C++. I wanted to see how it's done, whether I could make it properly versatile, and to really understand Monte Carlo methods: how they work, why they converge, and how to make them fast and trustworthy.

For anyone new to it, the idea behind Monte Carlo is simple. You simulate a random outcome, do it lots of times, and average the results. I've tried to build the engine to be completely general, so you can slap it on pretty much anything. If you can write your problem as "simulate one random outcome and give me a number", it plugs straight in, and the engine takes care of the rest: generating the randomness, running the samples and crunching the statistics. Every answer also comes with its own error bar, so you always know how much to trust it.

The core is up and running. At its heart is a fast random number generator which, I'll be honest, isn't actually random. It's a perfectly predictable formula doing a very convincing impression of randomness. I did something similar a few years back, generating random numbers on GPUs to introduce random disturbances into a boundary-layer simulation, mimicking the effect of atmospheric disturbances.

Real randomness has to come from something physical. Your processor actually has a source built in, based on tiny fluctuations in its electronics, and there are services like random.org that generate random numbers from atmospheric noise. Quantum mechanics is the gold standard, since some quantum events are believed to be fundamentally unpredictable. People have even used a quantum computer to generate certified random numbers, with some of the world's biggest supercomputers needed just to check the results. That's a wonderfully expensive way to flip a coin. If you're interested in that side of things, random.org goes into it in real depth, and this Computerphile video is a great watch too.

For simulation, though, faking it is actually the better option. It's much faster, and the same seed gives exactly the same results every time, which makes debugging far less painful. The one place real randomness is handy is picking that starting seed.

Add in some stable statistics and an estimator that always reports its own uncertainty, and you've got the foundations. For a first sanity check, I got it to estimate π, and it behaves exactly as the textbooks say it should. The error shrinks as 1/√N, and the 95% confidence intervals contain the true value 95% of the time. That last bit is the one I'm happiest with, because it shows the error bars are actually honest, not just that the answer happens to be close.

To prove it really does work on anything, I'm trying it out on three completely unrelated problems, each with a known answer I can check against:

Trajectory uncertainty. If you're not quite sure of the launch angle, speed, drag or wind, where will something actually land? The engine tries thousands of plausible combinations, solves the equations of motion for each one, and builds up the spread of possible landing spots. I'm checking the solver against the exact no-drag solution first.

Option pricing. An option's fair price is its average payoff across loads of possible market paths. I'm using Multilevel Monte Carlo, a neat trick that does most of the work on cheap, rough simulations and then tidies up with a few detailed ones. The answers get checked against the exact Black–Scholes formula.

Error-correction thresholds. Error-correcting codes only protect information if the error rate stays below a certain tipping point. The engine throws random errors at a code, runs a decoder, and counts how often it fails at different code sizes. The threshold shows up where those failure curves cross, and I can compare it with published values.

Three different fields, one engine underneath. I haven't made the branch public just yet, but I'm hoping to in the next few days, so keep an eye out. I'll post results and code here as each bit comes together.
