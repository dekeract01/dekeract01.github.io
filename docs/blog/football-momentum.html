Football Momentum

I found something interesting while watching the World Cup this summer. Every so often the broadcast would flash up a "momentum" graph, swinging back and forth between the two teams, and it kept me thinking: how do you even go about calculating that? As a fan, I can feel when a team has momentum. But when my friends and I started talking about the data behind it, we couldn't agree on what it actually means. How does a computer know which team is on top? And how would you work it out in real time?

So, naturally, I went looking for data. I saw that StatsBomb publishes match data, but unfortunately I could only find it for the 2022 World Cup, so 2022 it is. If the 2026 data does get released, I'm hoping to run the same scripts on it. Here's what's in there:

  • every pass, carry, shot and press
  • where on the pitch each one happened
  • a timestamp down to the second
  • around 235,000 events across the 64 matches

So what actually is momentum?

The first surprise (shocker, I know) was that there's no single answer. I ended up with five families of measure:

  • Territory: who has the ball, and where
  • Threat: how dangerous that possession actually is
  • Intensity: how hard each team is pressing
  • Dynamics: whether being on top makes you more likely to stay on top
  • Outcomes: what happens after a goal

For threat, I leaned on expected goals (xG), which gives every shot a probability of going in based on where and how it was taken. Shots are rare, though, so I also built a possession value model that scores every pass and carry by how much closer it brings a team to scoring. That's roughly what the broadcast graphs are doing behind the scenes.

Here's the fun bit: the measures don't always agree. Possession barely tracks how dangerous a team actually is, so "who's on top" depends heavily on how you choose to measure it. I'll be honest, that settled a few arguments with my mates.

But what about the result?

We all know that at the end of the day, the outcome is what matters. So what if I pick some other games? I support England, so of course I went straight for our quarter-final against France. I know it was only the quarter-finals, but I still remember that gut-wrenching feeling.

On paper, England were the better side. We had 57% of the passes, twice as many shots (16 to 8) and nearly three times the expected goals (2.35 to 0.84). And we still lost 2-1, with Kane's second penalty flying over late on. The painful twist is that most of our xG came from those two penalties. Take them out, and the two sides were almost level on chances (0.79 to 0.84). So England had more of the ball, but France made theirs count. Which is, I suppose, the whole point.

Put it all into a single broadcast-style momentum graph and the story's even clearer. France had a spell on top before Tchouaméni's screamer, then England were on top for most of the match. France had one short, sharp burst, and Giroud scored from it.

Testing it at the World Cup

Here's the catch with those broadcast graphs. They always swing about, but so does pure randomness. Flip a coin enough times and you'll get streaks that look an awful lot like momentum too. So the real question isn't whether the graph moves, it's whether momentum predicts anything.

This is where Monte Carlo comes in. I simulated 2,000 tournaments in a world with no momentum at all, where every team keeps the same number of shots but they're scattered at random through the match, and compared them with the real thing. The real data looks just like the simulated ones. In the real World Cup, 43.6% of a team's shots came within five minutes of its previous one, right in the middle of what the no-momentum world gives you (p = 0.53). And a team's threat in one minute tells you very little about the next few.

Goals do change matches, just not in the way you'd expect. Lining up all 169 goals from the tournament, teams tend to sit back after scoring, and the team that's just conceded pushes forward. That's pretty much the opposite of "a goal gives you momentum".

What's next

There's a sneaky problem with doing any of this in real time. A live system can only use what's already happened, whereas most of my smoothing so far quietly peeks at the minutes after each point as well. Fine for looking back at a finished match, but no good for a live graph. The momentum graph above is a first step, since it only looks backwards. The next step is a properly forward-looking model. I'm planning a self-exciting (Hawkes) process, where each dangerous attack briefly raises the chance of another one, a no-momentum baseline that accounts for the scoreline, and a much bigger dataset, to see whether there's a real but subtle momentum effect hiding in there after all. After that, I'd like to try the same approach on a full Premier League season, where there's far more data to unpack.

The code is now up on GitHub, including a notebook that walks through every step and makes all the figures above. I'll post results here as each bit comes together, so keep an eye out.
